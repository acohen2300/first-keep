## Peoples

| People | Unique soldier | Bonus | Wonder | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roman | Legionary (for the Swordsman) | Engineers: buildings go up 15% faster. | Colosseum | Legions did their own building: soldiers of three legions raised most of Hadrian's Wall from AD 122. Rome's legions fought with pilum and gladius, and also built the empire's roads, forts and walls: Hadrian's Wall was largely raised by legionaries from AD 122. The Flavian Amphitheatre, opened by Titus in AD 80 with a hundred days of games, seated about 50,000 Romans. |
| Han | Han crossbowman (for the Crossbowman) | Iron ploughs and new field systems: fields give 10% more food. | Weiyang Palace | The Han state made iron ploughshares from 117 BC, and new field systems spread; critics at the time called the state tools crude, so the gain here is modest. Han armies fielded crossbows by the tens of thousands: one arsenal list from 13 BC counts over 11,000, with bronze triggers cast to a standard pattern. Weiyang Palace, completed about 200 BC in Chang'an, was the seat of Han emperors for two centuries; its walls ran about 14 km. Only the platforms survive, so its look here is a reconstruction. |
| Persian | Immortal (for the Swordsman) | The Royal Road: caravans earn 20% more. | Apadana of Persepolis | Along the Royal Road, 111 staging posts linked Sardis to Susa, and relay riders carried messages across the empire. Herodotus says the Persian king's guard of 10,000 was never allowed to fall below strength, so the Greeks called them Immortals. They carried spear, bow and wicker shield. Darius I founded Persepolis in 518 BC; in its Apadana hall, delegations from across the empire brought tribute to the king. |
| Mongol | Mongol horse archer (for the Horse archer) | Every rider kept spare horses: everything on horseback is 10% faster. | Karakorum and the Silver Tree | Each rider kept three or four horses and swapped them to cover long distances without tiring them. A Mongol rider went to war with three or four horses and changed mounts to keep moving for days; the khans' armies outpaced every enemy on the continent. At Karakorum, about 1254, the Parisian smith Guillaume Boucher built Möngke Khan a silver tree that poured four drinks for the court, as the friar William of Rubruck reported. |
| Maurya | Maurya war elephant (for the War elephant) | The king held all horses and elephants: everything on horseback trains 25% faster. | Great Stupa at Sanchi | Megasthenes, a Greek envoy at the Maurya court about 300 BC, said horses and elephants belonged to the king alone. A Greek envoy at Chandragupta's court reported that horses and elephants were the king's monopoly; the royal corps was the largest of its day. Ashoka began the Great Stupa at Sanchi in the 3rd century BC; later rulers cased it in stone and added its four carved gateways. |
| Mali | Mali horseman (for the Cataphract) | The gold fields: gold gathered 15% faster. | Djinguereber Mosque | Mali held the Bambuk goldfields. Mansa Musa spent so much gold in Cairo in 1324 that its value there fell for years. Al-Umari (about 1340) reported that the Mansa of Mali kept a standing cavalry and gave his chiefs horses; Ibn Battuta saw mounted commanders with bows. The Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu is traditionally dated to 1327, built by order of Mansa Musa after his pilgrimage. It still stands, rebuilt in the 1570s. |

## The wild

| Find | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Treasure chest | Someone buried this and never came back. Kill the guards, then touch it. | People buried their wealth in troubled times; hoards like Hoxne in England were found centuries later. |
| Ruins | An old library in ruins, held by outlaws. Clear it and you find a scroll: one research of your age, free. | Abandoned cities held old knowledge: much of Greek science reached Europe through Arabic copies kept elsewhere. |
| Old cemetery | Grave goods, and grave robbers already at work. Clear them and the gold is yours. | Grave goods drew robbers in every age: almost every Egyptian tomb was robbed in antiquity. |
| Robbers' camp | Robbers who prey on caravans and villagers near the map edge. Break the camp and take its hoard. | Brigands haunted every trade route; merchants hired guards, and states hunted bandits along them. |
| Bear den | A bear and its stores. The meat is good too. | Bears and wolves were real dangers in the forests of Europe and Asia well into the Middle Ages. |
| Trade stop | A stop on an old trade route. Caravans sell goods here and buy spice. Clear its guards and build a Trade post to own it. | Spice did not grow in Europe: pepper, cinnamon and cloves came along the caravan and sea routes from Asia, and sold for many times what they cost where they grew. |
| Shrine | A holy site. Stand a priest by it for 10 s and it pays its keeper 15 gold a minute. Another side's priest can take it back. | Holy sites drew pilgrims and gifts, and rulers fought to hold them (Delphi, Jerusalem, Mecca, Bodh Gaya). |
| Vagabonds | Three drifters will fight for whoever pays 90 gold. A unit of yours has to be within 80. | Mercenaries are as old as armies: Xenophon's Ten Thousand were Greeks hired by a Persian prince. |

## Rules: what triggers what

| When | Then | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You start a game | Pick your civilisation (Roman, Han, Persian, Mongol, Maurya, Mali). The three computers take three of the others at random. Each civilisation has one unique soldier, one small bonus and its own wonder; the rest is shared. | A real people, but the same rules for all: no civilisation is stronger on paper. |
| You order a unit and the town is full | The order waits ("needs room") and nothing is lost. Room opens when a House, Apartment or Aqueduct finishes or a unit dies. | A town is as big as its shelter. |
| The screen | Top, one bar: food, wood, stone, gold, iron, then sound, pause and play, and speed. Under it: your age, the season and the clock on the left (gold when the next age or a research is ready: tap it for the Town Center), idle villagers beside it, and your score and rank on the right (tap for the table). The map icon sits top right (its − shrinks it again); Home bottom left when your Town Center is out of sight; Build bottom right. Bottom: People first (tap for people, army, research and how to play; − cancels one waiting to train, + trains one), then each job with − and +. + places an idle villager, and new villagers go to the job you picked. Build swaps the jobs for War, Walls, Trade, Culture and Home; ✕ brings the jobs back. A book on a building means research waits there. | Only what you act on stays in sight; the rest is a tap away. |
| Messages and sound | Every message is 10 words or fewer, one at a time. Tips and Messages can each be turned off; warnings stay on. Sound follows your phone's silent switch. | A phone game is played in short looks, often in company. |
| Room | You start with room for 5. A House adds 5, an Apartment 20, an Aqueduct 15. At most 150 people: 60 villagers and workers, 90 soldiers. | Town Centers the game readable on a phone, and every side has the same limits. |
| The War Council ladder | Four researches, each opening the next. Engagement: stances. Battalions: six, Alpha to Zeta; set what each holds, by type; new soldiers fill them, the rest are the reserve. Formations: Line, Column, Wedge, Square. Muster (Gunpowder): a battalion's missing soldiers in one order, twice the cost, a quarter longer, the time shared by every building that trains them. The computers climb it too. | Rome levied whole legions at once; Maurice of Nassau's drill (1590s) made trained formations, and armies like the New Model Army (1645) were raised as one force. |
| Villager jobs: Food, Wood, Stone, Gold, Iron | The − and + under each job move one villager off or on. Food takes a free field first if one is within 160, else the nearest berries, game or fish (fish only within 150 of a dock). | You decide who does what. |
| Hunting | Villagers on Food hunt deer and boar near home: they throw javelins, the animal falls, and they butcher it where it lies (a deer gives 500, a boar 850). Boar fight back. | Meat came before fields everywhere. |
| You build | Press where it goes: the building waits above your finger and snaps flush beside your other buildings, side by side; buildings that train keep a walking lane around them. Green fits, red says why not. Let go, drag to adjust, then tap Build. Houses, fields and gates offer the next spot beside. | Rows that line up, no hidden gaps, and you never cover what you are placing. |
| Everyone eats | Every minute each villager eats a share (3.75 food), a foot soldier 1.5 shares, a rider or elephant 3 (horse fodder), a cart 2. One villager gathering food feeds about 8. With no food, work and damage drop by a quarter and nothing can be trained; nobody starves. | Real towns and armies ate every day; an army's horses ate more than its men. |
| You hold a vassal | Beat their army and stand at their Town Center, then Demand surrender; or, as the Fortress's ally and twice its strength, offer to be its overlord. Every 10 s you take the tax (light 10%, fair 20%, heavy 35% of what they gathered). Loyalty heads for 20 + your buildings near them (castle 20, town 15, market 10, tower, post, business center 6, field 2, others 3) + your soldiers there (0.5 each, up to 15) + enemies you killed near them (1 each, up to 20, fading) − the tax's resentment (0, 10, 30). Unrest moves by the tax push (0.3, 1.5, 4) minus loyalty/25. At 60 loyalty they send gifts; at 50 you get a quarter of their trade profit. | Rulers who protected and built up their subjects were paid more and kept them; those who only squeezed them faced revolt. |
| Unrest reaches 100 | A rebellion: a third of their villagers take up arms and their army turns on you. Kill three quarters of them and it is crushed (the rebels are lost); if they hold out 4 minutes, they are free. With two or more vassals, every minute there is a chance that all the restless ones rise together: the chance grows with their unrest and their number. | Overstretched empires fell to joint revolts. |
| You take down a building | Half its cost comes back; whatever was waiting in it comes back in full. Cancel in the first 15% of the build returns all of it. The Town Center cannot be taken down. | Salvage, never a way to make money. |
| Soldiers destroy a building | Its destroyer takes 25% of what it cost. Taking a building down yourself pays the enemy nothing. | Spoils of war, never worth more than the fight. Every side is paid the same way. |
| A Town Center falls | Whoever struck the last blow takes the whole stockpile and 25% of the cost of every building left standing. | Conquerors took the treasury. A rich stockpile is a target. |
| Score | Always on screen, top right; tap it for the table. Score = People (civilians held, cost ÷ 5) + Army (soldiers held, cost ÷ 5) + Tech (research and ages paid, ÷ 5) + Buildings (standing, cost ÷ 5) + Wealth (everything gathered, traded, found and plundered, ÷ 10). Losses come off because you no longer hold them. | Every building and every worker counts toward something, the same way for every side. |
| A timed game | Help → Game: Conquest (no limit), 30 minutes or 60 minutes. At the bell the highest score wins. | A guaranteed end, and a reason to build more than an army. |
| A wonder is finished | Every side is told. If it still stands 8 minutes later, its owner wins. The computers attack a rival's wonder at once. | A wonder shows a state at its height; it is also a target. |
| Finds in the wild | Each corner has the same: deer, a boar, a treasure chest under wolves, ruins held by bandits (a free research of your age), an old cemetery with grave robbers, a bear den, vagabonds to hire. Robbers' camps sit at the four map edges; four shrines stand on the great lake. Guards stay near what they guard and attack whoever comes close. Kill them, then touch the find with any unit. | Taking what is far means fighting for it. The map is the same from every corner. |

| Shrines | A priest that stands by a shrine for 10 s takes it. A shrine pays its keeper 15 gold a minute. Another side's priest can take it back. | Rulers fought to hold holy sites; pilgrims brought gifts. |

Near an enemy building it reads their stores, age, research and army. | States watched for spies as long as they have had secrets. |

Town Center, towers, walls, gates and Courthouses cannot be sabotaged. | A spy buys time; it never wins a war alone. |

| Unit lines and tiers | Some research upgrades a unit in place and renames it: Spearman → Hoplite (Phalanx) → Pikeman (Pike square); Swordsman → Man-at-arms; Archer → Longbowman; Chariot → Horseman (Cavalry); Cataphract → Knight (Knighthood); Arquebusier → Musketeer (Musket). A horse archer always skirmishes. The computers use them too once they research it: their shooters skirmish, their foot at home hold a wall. | The phalanx and the shield wall were slow and hard to break; skirmishers and horse archers fought by not being caught (Carrhae, 53 BC). |

Send ¼ sends a quarter of each type, nearest first; All sends everyone; with Battalions, each one by name; ⋯ for the rally point, scout and spy; ✕ to cancel. Tap an enemy building or soldier: pick how many of each type, then Attack, with the odds. | Tap bare ground: pick how many of each type, then Send. Tap an enemy building or soldier: the same, then Attack, with the odds. With battalions, pick one first. |

| Soldiers come within 70 of a villager | The villager runs into the nearest tower with room (up to 5) or the Town Center (up to 8 arrows) and adds an arrow, +1 damage each. | You pay for an age at the Town Center | Bronze 40 s, Classical 60 s, Medieval 80 s, Gunpowder 100 s. It opens the next buildings, soldiers and research. | Time is the price of progress. |

| You send an emissary (Bronze age) | It rides to the Fortress. At war: killed. Allied elsewhere: sent back. If you are its biggest threat and clearly stronger: sent back with nothing. | The Fortress is your ally | Every 45 s it sends part of what it has above 200. It sends soldiers when your town is attacked and its own is safe. | An ally who shares is worth having. |
| A computer decides to attack | It compares its power with the enemy's: odds = r²/(1+r²), the square law of aimed fire (Lanchester, 1916, a modern model). It goes only if the odds beat its nerve: Raiders .5, Rivals .65, the Fortress never. A wave that stalls goes home. | A rule of thumb, not history: ancient melee followed the square law less well. |
| Trees stand in the way | Forest blocks people, carts and armies. A tree can be cut only from open ground beside it, so a forest is worked from its edge inward; a felled tree opens its ground. Roads halve the slowing. | A wooden building catches fire | Sabotage, summer lightning or sparks from a bakery or smithy. It burns down in about half a minute; two villagers run with buckets; it can spread to wooden buildings next to it. Stone does not burn. | Towns of timber and thatch burned often: London in 1212 and 1666. |

Up to 12 soldiers garrison each stop. You set what the stop wants. | Whoever held the road taxed the trade on it. |

With nobody on it, it does not rise. A line of wall gets two builders who work down it. When done, a builder goes back to what he was doing. | Building takes hands: a far-off site is only as safe as its builder, and the army has to escort it. |
| No fog | You see the whole map, and so does everyone else. Only enemy spies stay hidden until something of yours is close. Night still shortens how far soldiers look for a target. | Your call: the map open, the same for every side. |
| A wall or building stands in the way | People walk around it. If nothing can get through, soldiers attack the wall in the way; villagers wait. | Walls delay; they do not stop an army with time. |
