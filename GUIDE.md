## Peoples

| People | Unique soldier | Bonus | Wonder | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roman | Legionary (for the Swordsman) | Engineers: buildings go up 15% faster. | Colosseum | Legions did their own building: soldiers of three legions raised most of the Wall of Hadrian from AD 122. The manipular legion, in three lines that relieved each other, beat the phalanx at Cynoscephalae (197 BC) and ruled for five centuries. Game: it replaces the swordsman of the Bronze age, three centuries before its time, so a Roman player has a Roman army from the start. The Flavian Amphitheatre, opened by Titus in AD 80 with a hundred days of games, seated about 50,000 Romans. |
| Han | Han crossbowman (for the Crossbowman) | Iron ploughs and new field systems: fields give 10% more food. | Weiyang Palace | The Han state made iron ploughshares from 117 BC, and new field systems spread; critics at the time called the state tools crude, so the gain here is modest. Han armies fielded crossbows by the tens of thousands: one arsenal list from 13 BC counts over 11,000, with bronze triggers cast to a standard pattern. Weiyang Palace, completed about 200 BC in Changan, was the seat of Han emperors for two centuries; its walls ran about 14 km. Only the platforms survive, so its look here is a reconstruction. |
| Persian | Immortal (for the Swordsman) | The Royal Road: caravans earn 20% more. | Apadana of Persepolis | Along the Royal Road, 111 staging posts linked Sardis to Susa, and relay riders carried messages across the empire. Herodotus says the Persian royal guard of 10,000 was never allowed to fall below strength, so the Greeks called them Immortals. They carried spear, bow and wicker shield (about 500 BC). Game: it replaces the swordsman of the Bronze age, earlier than its time, so a Persian player has a Persian army from the start. Darius I founded Persepolis in 518 BC; in its Apadana hall, delegations from across the empire brought tribute to the king. |
| Mongol | Mongol horse archer (for the Horse archer) | Every rider kept spare horses: everything on horseback is 10% faster. | Karakorum and the Silver Tree | Each rider kept three or four horses and swapped them to cover long distances without tiring them. A Mongol rider went to war with three or four horses and changed mounts to keep moving for days; the armies of the khans outpaced every enemy on the continent. At Karakorum, about 1254, the Parisian smith Guillaume Boucher built Möngke Khan a silver tree that poured four drinks for the court, as the friar William of Rubruck reported. |
| Maurya | Maurya war elephant (for the War elephant) | The king held all horses and elephants: everything on horseback trains 25% faster. | Great Stupa at Sanchi | Megasthenes, a Greek envoy at the Maurya court about 300 BC, said horses and elephants belonged to the king alone. A Greek envoy at the court of Chandragupta reported that horses and elephants were a royal monopoly; the royal corps was the largest of its day. Ashoka began the Great Stupa at Sanchi in the 3rd century BC; later rulers cased it in stone and added its four carved gateways. |
| Mali | Mali horseman (for the Cataphract) | The gold fields: gold gathered 15% faster. | Djinguereber Mosque | Mali held the Bambuk goldfields. Mansa Musa spent so much gold in Cairo in 1324 that its value there fell for years. Al-Umari (about 1340) reported that the Mansa of Mali kept a standing cavalry and gave his chiefs horses; Ibn Battuta saw mounted commanders with bows. The Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu is traditionally dated to 1327, built by order of Mansa Musa after his pilgrimage. It still stands, rebuilt in the 1570s. |

| Bear den | A bear and its stores. The meat is good too. | Bears and wolves were real dangers in the forests of Europe and Asia well into the Middle Ages. |
| Trade stop | A stop on an old trade route. Caravans sell goods here and buy spice. Clear its guards and build a Trade post to own it. | Spice did not grow in Europe: pepper, cinnamon and cloves came along the caravan and sea routes from Asia, and sold for many times what they cost where they grew. |
| Shrine | The one holy site, at the foot of the Throne. Stand a priest by it for 10 s and it pays its keeper 15 gold a minute. A priest of another side can take it back. | Holy sites drew pilgrims and gifts, and rulers fought to hold them (Delphi, Jerusalem, Mecca, Bodh Gaya). |
| Vagabonds | Three drifters will fight for whoever pays 90 gold. A unit of yours has to be within 80. | Mercenaries are as old as armies: the Ten Thousand of Xenophon were Greeks hired by a Persian prince. |
| Mercenaries | A free company camped by the road: six swords for 240 gold, for three minutes, then they move on. | The free companies of the 1300s sold whole armies by the season: the White Company under Hawkwood hired itself to whoever paid in Italy. |
| Fallen star | Iron from the sky. Before smelting, this was where iron came from; the dagger of Tutankhamun was forged from it. | Meteoric iron was worked before anyone could smelt it: the dagger of Tutankhamun and Inuit tools were made from fallen stars. |

## Rules: what triggers what

| When | Then | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You send soldiers somewhere | Each soldier shows its state in the Army list: Rallying, Marching, Fighting, Holding or Routed. A ring on the map marks the spot with the time left; tap the ring to pick that group. Recall brings a marching group home. Follow keeps the camera on the picked soldiers. | You always see what each soldier is doing and where it will stand. |
| An enemy group you can see heads for your buildings | The bar turns red: how many, and seconds until they arrive. Tap it to jump to them. | Time to pick your soldiers before the first hit. |
| You start a game | Pick your civilisation (Roman, Han, Persian, Mongol, Maurya, Mali). The three computers take three of the others at random. Each civilisation has one unique soldier, one small bonus and its own wonder; the rest is shared. | A real people, but the same rules for all: no civilisation is stronger on paper. |
| You order a unit and the town is full | The order waits ("needs room") and nothing is lost. Room opens when a House, Apartment or Aqueduct finishes or a unit dies. | A town is as big as its shelter. |
| The screen | Top, one bar: food, wood, stone, gold, iron, then sound, pause and play, and speed. Under it: your age, the season and the clock on the left (gold when the next age or a research is ready: tap it for the Town Center), idle villagers beside it, and your score and rank on the right (tap for the table). The map icon sits top right (its − shrinks it again); Home bottom left when your Town Center is out of sight; Build bottom right. Bottom: People first (tap for people, army, research and how to play; − cancels one waiting to train, + trains one), then each job with − and +. + places an idle villager, and new villagers go to the job you picked. Build swaps the jobs for War, Walls, Trade, Culture and Home; ✕ brings the jobs back. A book on a building means research waits there. | Only what you act on stays in sight; the rest is a tap away. |
| Messages and sound | Every message is 10 words or fewer, one at a time. Tips and Messages can each be turned off; warnings stay on. Sound follows the silent switch of your phone. | A phone game is played in short looks, often in company. |
| Room | One population: your people are your people, worker or soldier, any mix. Only homes hold them: you start with room for 5; a House adds 5, an Apartment 20 (it needs an Aqueduct); Timber framing, Fired brick, Town charter, Plumbing and Waterworks add more. The phone sets a ceiling: 180 people, 120 of them soldiers. An army eats: that is its real limit. | Keeps the game readable on a phone, and every side has the same limits. |
| Limits | Each building has a limit that grows with the age: farms 6 at first, up to 12; one Barracks, up to 3; most other buildings one. A training building makes what its line asks: two of them are two lines. Every side has the same limits. | A town can only feed so many fields, and a barracks never housed an army: it trained one. |
| The War Council ladder | Four researches, each opening the next. Engagement: shooters step back from a foe on top of them. Battalions: six, Alpha to Zeta; set what each holds, by type; new soldiers fill them, the rest are the reserve. Formations: Line, Wedge, Square, and your armies march in column. Muster (Gunpowder): the missing soldiers of a battalion in one order, twice the cost, a quarter longer, the time shared by every building that trains them. The computers climb it too. | Rome levied whole legions at once; the drill of Maurice of Nassau (1590s) made trained formations, and armies like the New Model Army (1645) were raised as one force. |
| Villager jobs: Food, Wood, Stone, Gold, Iron | The − and + under each job move one villager off or on: + takes an idle villager, else the one earning least elsewhere; − sends home the one earning least. Each worker takes the find that brings home most a minute: its load over the time to fill it and carry it home and back (fish only within 150 of a dock). A worker on a field, tree farm, pasture or quarry stays on it. A worker on a wild find that runs out looks only for the same kind of find within its own sight (gold rock to gold rock); when none is in sight it waits at the drop-off for your order. Tap the lit job tile again to turn the default job off: new villagers then wait at the Town Center. Stop and Dismiss sit on a villager card; Stop all and Idle to me on the Town Center card. | You decide who does what, and nobody wanders off. |
| Hunting | Your villagers do not hunt unless you send them (Food on a deer or boar): they throw javelins, the animal falls, and they butcher it where it lies (a deer gives 500, a boar 850). An animal that attacks a villager is fought by that villager and the two nearest in sight. The computers hunt on their own: they have no finger to send a hunter. | Meat came before fields everywhere; a wolf at the door was the fight of all. |
| You build | Press where it goes: the building waits above your finger over the build grid and snaps flush beside your other buildings, side by side; buildings that train keep a walking lane around them. Green fits, red says why not. Let go, drag to adjust, then tap Build. The ghost stays for the next of the same kind until the limit or the purse says no. A cut-out tree farm shows stumps: tap it to replant at half the cost. A kind at its limit dims with a check in the Build card. | Rows that line up, no hidden gaps, and you never cover what you are placing. |
| Everyone eats | Every minute each villager eats a share (3.75 food), a foot soldier 1.5 shares, a rider or elephant 3 (horse fodder), a cart 2. One villager gathering food feeds about 8. With no food, work and damage drop by 25% and nothing can be trained; nobody starves. | Real towns and armies ate every day; the horses of an army ate more than its men. |
| You hold a vassal | Beat their army and stand at their Town Center, then Demand surrender; or, as the ally of a side and twice its strength, offer to be its overlord. Every 10 s you take the tax (light 10%, fair 20%, heavy 35% of what they gathered). Loyalty heads for 20 + your buildings near them (castle 20, town 15, market 10, tower, post, business center 6, field 2, others 3) + your soldiers there (0.5 each, up to 15) + enemies you killed near them (1 each, up to 20, fading) − the resentment of the tax (0, 10, 30). Unrest moves by the tax push (0.3, 1.5, 4) minus loyalty/25. At 60 loyalty they send gifts; at 50 you get a quarter of their trade profit. | Rulers who protected and built up their subjects were paid more and kept them; those who only squeezed them faced revolt. |
| Unrest reaches 100 | A rebellion: a third of their villagers take up arms and their army turns on you. Kill three quarters of them and it is crushed (the rebels are lost); if they hold out 4 minutes, they are free. With two or more vassals, every minute there is a chance that all the restless ones rise together: the chance grows with their unrest and their number. | Overstretched empires fell to joint revolts. |
| You take down a building | Half its cost comes back; whatever was waiting in it comes back in full. Cancel in the first 15% of the build returns all of it. The Town Center cannot be taken down. | Salvage, never a way to make money. |
| Soldiers destroy a building | Its destroyer takes 25% of what it cost. Taking a building down yourself pays the enemy nothing. | Spoils of war, never worth more than the fight. Every side is paid the same way. |
| A Town Center falls | Whoever struck the last blow takes the whole stockpile and 25% of the cost of every building left standing. | Conquerors took the treasury. A rich stockpile is a target. |
| Score | Always on screen, top right; tap it for the table. Score = People (civilians held, cost ÷ 5) + Army (soldiers held, cost ÷ 5) + Tech (research and ages paid, ÷ 5) + Buildings (standing, cost ÷ 5) + Wealth (everything gathered, traded, found and plundered, ÷ 10). Losses come off because you no longer hold them. | Every building and every worker counts toward something, the same way for every side. |
| A timed game | Help → Game: Conquest (no limit), 15, 30 or 60 minutes. At the bell the highest score wins. A scenario sets the start: the Siege, the Silk Road, Last Keep. | A guaranteed end, and a reason to build more than an army. |
| A wonder is finished | Every side is told. If it still stands 8 minutes later, its owner wins. The computers attack the wonder of a rival at once. | A wonder shows a state at its height; it is also a target. |
| Finds in the wild | Each corner has the same: deer, a boar, a treasure chest under wolves, ruins held by bandits (a free research of your age), an old cemetery with grave robbers, a bear den, vagabonds to hire. The camps of the robbers sit at the four map edges; the shrine stands inside the Throne. Guards stay near what they guard and attack whoever comes close. Kill them, then touch the find with any unit. | Taking what is far means fighting for it. The map is the same from every corner. |
| Robbers | Robbers attack caravans and villagers within 260 of their camp. Break the camp and its hoard is yours. | Merchants hired guards because bandits were real. |
| The shrine | One shrine, at the foot of the Throne. A priest that stands by it for 10 s takes it, and it pays its keeper 15 gold a minute. A priest of another side can take it back. | Rulers fought to hold holy sites; pilgrims brought gifts. |
| Wayfarers | Every 4 minutes a wayfarer walks in from one map edge, in turn, toward the other side. The first unit of any side that meets him brings home a free villager. | Towns grew by newcomers. |
| The fantasy toggle | Help → World: real (default) or fantasy. Fantasy swaps the guards for dire wolves, goblins, skeletons and trolls, and the finds pay half again as much. Everything else stays the same. | Your call: history by default, legend if you like. |
| Courthouse and spies | The Courthouse trains spies; its guards shoot the enemy spies they see, within bowshot (126). A spy is seen by soldiers only within 45 and by towers, the Town Center and Courthouses within 140. Near an enemy building it reads their stores, age, research and army. | States watched for spies as long as they have had secrets. |
| Sabotage | A spy sent to an enemy building is used up. A field, drop-off, house or hospital loses 60% of its health; a building that trains or researches stops for 40 s and loses the job in hand. Town Center, towers, walls, gates and Courthouses cannot be sabotaged. | A spy buys time; it never wins a war alone. |
| Market | Sell 100 of a resource or 10 of a good for 80% of its worth (Coinage 90%), or buy for 125%, at the going price. The going price answers the stock: what is sold piles up and the price falls, what is bought runs short and it rises, by the demand curve (bread and wood move little, cloth and spice a lot); a glut or a shortage wears off over a couple of minutes. Spice is the only good it sells. The same prices for every side; a round trip always loses, and a trade stop pays more. | The margin is the cut of the merchant. |
| Trade with an ally | A caravan with no route runs from your Market to the Market or Town Center of an ally and earns distance² ÷ 16000, at most 80 gold, on arrival, then comes home. Kill an enemy caravan and its load is yours. | Silk-road pay grew with distance, and so did the danger. |
| Unit lines and tiers | Some research upgrades a unit in place and renames it: Spearman → Hoplite (Phalanx) → Pikeman (Pike square); Swordsman → Man-at-arms; Archer → Longbowman; Chariot → Horseman (Cavalry); Cataphract → Knight (Knighthood); Arquebusier → Musketeer (Musket). Every one you have is upgraded at once. | Armies changed their kit, not their soldiers. |
| Counters | Spears beat anything on horseback; riders beat archers and siege; archers beat foot; javelins beat archers; crossbows and guns beat armour; swords beat foot and buildings; ballistae beat elephants. The unit cards say who beats whom. | Every weapon had its answer. |
| Siege | Rams, catapults, trebuchets and bombards attack only buildings. Siege towers and ballistae shoot soldiers too. Arrows glance off rams and siege towers. | Soldiers hit walls slowly; real armies brought engines. |
| Temper and shape | Two dials in the Army card, for all of you or one battalion. Temper: Hold, fight only what comes within reach, no chase, steadier nerve. Guard, fight what comes; shooters step back from anyone who closes in. Press, chase and charge: blows 10% harder, nerve breaks 30% faster. Once Engagement is researched, shooters under Hold or Guard step back from anyone who comes within 45 while they reload; a horse archer always does. Shape, once Formations is researched: Line, as they stand. Wedge, riders hit 15% harder, take 10% more. Square, 10% less damage, 30% less from riders, 20% slower. An army marches in column (15% faster, 10% more damage taken) and stands to its shape when it meets a foe. The computers keep Guard. | Armies marched in column and deployed into line to fight: the Roman three lines and the medieval three battles, van, main body and rear, were the same idea. Skirmishers and horse archers fought by not being caught (Carrhae, 53 BC). |
| Nerve and the rout | Every soldier has nerve. A blow drains it by the share of health it takes (half again from the side, twice from behind); a comrade falling beside it drains it by one share of the group, so a group breaks when it has lost 30% of its number; being outnumbered three to two drains it slowly. Quiet lets it settle back, 15 s to recover most of it, faster by a wall or tower of its own and by a priest. At nothing it routs: runs for home 8 s, takes more harm, and can be taken prisoner. | Most real battles ended in a rout, not a last man: armies broke when losses reached a tenth to a third (Dupuy, Understanding War, 1987). |
| The song | There is no music in life. Life is the music. You will notice a rhythm and a beat emerging from your kingdom: that is its own song, composed by your decisions. Every stroke sounds when it lands, at the pace of the find it works; crews fall into step on their own; the birds, the wind and the thunder sit on top. Nobody else writes a note of it. | Work songs kept crews in time long before metronomes: sea shanties, the sowing songs of the fields, the smith striking on the beat. The game plays only what happens. |
| Hit from behind | A foot soldier or rider struck from behind the line it holds takes 30% more damage, and its nerve shakes more. It counts only when the man cannot turn to meet it (engaged with another, slipped, or broken) and no comrade stands in the way: a big group is turned only at its ends. Shooters do not get it. Splitting the army to catch a line from two sides pays. | Armies lost most men when their line was turned: Cannae, 216 BC. |
| Heavy blows | Elephants, cataphracts and trebuchets drive what they hit back a few steps, and it flashes white. | Elephants and armoured charges broke formations by weight. |
| The send strip | Tap open ground: two buttons at your finger. The sword sends soldiers there: Idle, All, or a squad by name. The people button builds there, sends idle villagers there, or sets how many workers stand there. ⋯ for more about the place; ✕ to cancel. Tap an enemy building or soldier with soldiers picked: they attack. | Commands should cost one touch. |
| The Natives and the Throne | The fifth side holds the Throne, a citadel on the island of the great lake, reached by four causeways. Their regard for you starts at 20: gifts raise it (each buys less than the last, as the Danegeld did), blows on them lower it, and it drifts back with time. At 40 they leave you be; at 70 an emissary can make them allies and their gate opens. Pits and nets wait on the causeways; a scout within 60 finds them. A net takes a prisoner: pay the ransom within 3 minutes or they join the Natives. From minute 10, at random times 3 to 8 minutes apart, outlaws from a camp raid a side at war with the Natives, the least regarded most often, never the same side twice running. | Rome paid frontier peoples to stay quiet; ransom ran through medieval warfare; pits and snares are the oldest defences there are. |
| The Throne | Break it down and it is yours at a third of its strength; or, as the ally of the Natives, stand 8 soldiers inside for 60 s and they swear to you. Whoever holds it takes 10% of the gathering of every other side and 50 score a minute, and every other side turns hostile. Hold it 15 minutes in a 60-minute game (less in a shorter one) and you win. | The wonder race and the throne fight in one place: it gives the hours a direction and the computers a reason to fight each other. |
| The ground remembers | Where people fell, where buildings burned and where villagers walk a lot, the ground shows it. It is for looks only and is not saved. | Battlefields and trodden roads stayed visible for years. |
| Hold ground | In the Army card, Hold ground makes the picked battalion fight only what comes within reach and not chase. | A line that chases a lone rider breaks itself. |
| Temple: Zeal | With a Temple and a priest near the battalion, Zeal costs 60 gold and makes it hit 20% harder for 45 s. Once every 2 minutes per side. Sacred games halve the cost and shorten the rest. | Belief steadied armies, but only so far. |
| Hospital and medics | Soldiers within 90 of a Hospital or 55 of a medic heal 3 health a second. The best one in reach counts. Medicine makes it 50% faster. | Care near the camp saved more men than the field surgeon. |
| A building of yours is damaged: tap it, then Repair | A villager mends it: a full repair takes three times the build time and costs a quarter of the building, paid as it goes. | Mending costs less than rebuilding, and it takes hands. |
| Soldiers come within 70 of a villager | The villager runs into the nearest tower with room (up to 5) or the Town Center (up to 8 arrows) and adds an arrow, +1 damage each. It comes out when no enemy soldier is within 150 of it. A tower also sounds the horn when foes first come in range. | People sheltered inside a fortress and helped defend it. |
| You pay for an age at the Town Center | Bronze 40 s, Classical 60 s, Medieval 80 s, Gunpowder 100 s. It opens the next buildings, soldiers and research. | Time is the price of progress. |
| You hit someone you are at peace with or allied to | It is an act of war: war is declared, an alliance breaks. | Same rule for all four sides. |
| You send an emissary (Bronze age) | It rides to a court. At war with you and not asked for peace: killed. Allied elsewhere: sent back. Peace or an alliance is answered by their odds: a side that is winning wants more of the war; a side that needs no ally sends you home. Their emissaries to you are answered by you, on the Relations side of your Town Center. | Envoys were heard or hanged by the strength behind them. |
| An ally | An ally sends soldiers when your town is attacked and its own is safe, and opens its Market to your caravans. Twice its strength, you may offer to be its overlord. | An ally who shares is worth having. |
| A computer decides to attack | It compares its power with that of the enemy: odds = r²/(1+r²), the square law of aimed fire (Lanchester, 1916, a modern model). It goes only if the odds beat its nerve: Raiders .5, Rivals .65, the Fortress .85 and only when it sees a chance. A wave that stalls goes home. | A rule of thumb, not history: ancient melee followed the square law less well. |
| Trees stand in the way | Forest blocks people, carts and armies. A tree can be cut only from open ground beside it, so a forest is worked from its edge inward; a felled tree opens its ground. Hold and drag a box over trees to send villagers to cut a road. | Old forests were barriers: roads were cut through them, and armies kept to the roads. |
| The ground remembers the weather | The air has a temperature, by the season and the hour, and the sky runs in spells: a wet minute is likelier after a wet one. Rain soaks the ground and it dries over minutes; wet ground is mud (marching up to 44% slower, deeper by water), snow falls below freezing and melts by the degree (marching up to 38% slower; engines slide and shoot short), wet ground freezes to ice (men slip). Fire follows the wetness: it hardly catches after rain and races in a drought. Frost and rain take 15% off blades. Each fades over days. The Library (Weather line), Stable, Barracks and Smithy research the answers: roads and drains, horseshoes and crampons, cloaks, tempering and oiling, levees against floods. | Napoleon lost more to mud and cold than to armies; Agincourt was won in a ploughed field after a week of rain. |
| Research comes in lines | Each building teaches a few lines; only the next step of a line shows, and it needs the step before, sometimes one elsewhere (Plate needs Bellows). Cost and time follow the weight of the step in history, 1 to 10. The book on each step gives the date and what it changed. | The real tree: nobody built a blast furnace before a bellows, and the plough mattered more than the sickle. |
| The Squad room and pausing | The chip at the left of the job row turns the row into the Squad room (once Command is researched): + makes a squad (Alpha to Delta, four at most), the tile picks one, and − and + under each kind fill its plan from the soldiers at home. A squad card shows its plan at home and its health and standing out on a mission. Opening any card pauses the game and closing it carries on (Help: Pause on cards, off if you like). | Thumbs on the map, not in a menu: a phone is not a desk. |
| The War room: four missions | The chip again turns the row into the War room: a crosshair goes where you tap; Go sends the picked squad (or every idle soldier) on it as a mission with a promised time, and the row shows each squad out: its time to arrive, then FIGHTING with its odds in colour, then done. A tile flashing red is losing: open it to Reinforce from the idle reserve, or Abort. A tap on the map offers the same in two buttons: the sword sends Idle, All or a squad there; the people button builds there, sends idle villagers there or sets workers there. | A plan you can read: time, odds and who is where. The weather on the screen is counted in the time; a storm that comes later is bad luck. |
| Emergency: shelter and battle stations | The Army card and the red attack bar open it. Shelter puts villagers in the sturdiest shelter first, Battle stations sends soldiers into buildings (they add arrows) and a line toward the danger, All hands does both. Resume sends villagers back to work; soldiers stay until you give them an order. | One rule for everyone: the strongest walls first, the nearest hands first. |
| Teach | Research Veterans at the Barracks, build a School: a soldier of an older kind can be taught a newer one (spear to sword, archer to crossbow, catapult to trebuchet). It stands frozen at its post for the lesson; tuition is 40 gold an age, plus the difference in cost. Four learn at a time per School. Fire arrows, burning shot and petards set wooden buildings alight; rain halves the chance. Soldiers resting by a Castle, Barracks or Town Center heal. | Marius retrained his veterans rather than raising new legions; the Spanish army kept wards for its garrisons from 1500. |
| The seasons turn | Each lasts 3 minutes. Spring: marching ×0.85, fields ×1; Summer: marching ×1, fields ×1.1; Autumn: marching ×1, fields ×1.25; Winter: marching ×0.8, fields ×0.4. Roads halve the slowing. Each spring sheep are shorn, lambs born, and the harvest of the year set (about one year in six poor, one in twenty failed). | Real farming years: mud and cold slowed armies, and the harvest decided the year. |
| The weather runs in spells | A wet minute is likelier after a wet one. Rain: the ground softens to mud, deepest where the feet go (ruts hold water; Roads mend it), bows reach 15% less, fire hardly catches. Snow at 1 °C and below: the ground deepens, sight shortens. Below freezing wet ground turns to ice, thick as the square root of the cold-time; ice is slickest just under freezing and grips better in deep cold; a step that asks more grip than the ground gives slips. What melts wets the ground: a thaw makes mud. Drought after dry warm days: fields give 30% less, fire catches at a touch. Frost: stone buildings go up at 50% speed, the ground hardens. | Wet roads were mud, wet bowstrings sagged, dry thatch burned, and mortar does not set in frost. Gabriel and Neumann (1962) showed rain runs in spells; Stefan (1891) gave the ice; Jennings (2018) the degree snow begins; Bowden and Hughes (1939) why ice is slick. |
| A wooden building catches fire | Sabotage, summer lightning, sparks from a smithy, or fire arrows and burning shot. It burns down in about half a minute; two villagers run with buckets. A fire grows as the square of its age, and the heat a neighbour receives falls with the gap between the walls (a quarter at 10, none past 40); so a young fire, or one a street away, rarely spreads. Stone does not burn. | Towns of timber and thatch burned often: London in 1212 and 1666. The square of its age is the design fire of NFPA 72; the fall-off is the view factor of radiant heat in BR 187. |
| Sickness | Now and then, more in summer and winter, a case arrives; it spreads through the town as real sickness does: each case makes about 2 more while people are well to catch it, fewer as the sick recover (the SIR model). Clean water, sewers and quarantine halve the spread. The sick work and fight at 60% and a few die; a Hospital or medics cure them. Plague comes along the trade routes in some springs and spreads 1.5× faster. | Summer flux and winter fever were the yearly toll; plague followed trade. Kermack and McKendrick wrote the arithmetic of an epidemic in 1927: a sickness grows while each case makes more than one, and burns out as it runs short of the well. |
| How news reaches you | You know what you see, what your people hear and what a messenger brings. Out of sight, a fight near your people is heard (which way, not what); anything else waits for a messenger, on foot or on horseback, and only if your post reaches that far: Runners your near lands, the Royal Road all your lands, Relay stations your allies, Post riders the whole board, News sheets the ages of the other courts. The sky you always see; Weather signs tell the next one a minute ahead. | Before the telegraph (1840s) news travelled at the speed of a horse: the news of the Battle of New Orleans (January 1815) reached Washington after the peace was already signed. |
| The whole board is at peace | When no two courts are at war and no notice runs, the board is at peace. After 90 s the computers stand their spare soldiers down to the fields; after a year the Enlightenment opens to every court. The Games are held at the Throne every 2 years, but only under this peace: war cancels them. Peace from a court that wants more war can be bought with gold (tribute). | The Olympic truce (ekecheiria) stopped the wars of Greece for the festival; Æthelred bought peace from the Danes in 991 with 10,000 pounds of silver, the Danegeld. |
| The Games | Four events: the footrace, the wrestling, the javelin, the chariot race. Each court sends the best it has in the field; the odds go with the square of that strength, as battle does. A win is a laurel, counted toward the Wellbeing crown. | Olympia, from 776 BC: the stadion footrace, wrestling (pale), the pentathlon with its javelin, and the four-horse chariot race (tethrippon) from 680 BC. Winners took a wreath of olive and a lifetime of honour at home. |
| The crowns | When a match ends, three crowns are pointed to: Wealth (all a court gathered and holds), War (its kills) and Wellbeing (its people in health, its places of care and recreation, its laurels), and an overall crown from the three. A match that ends in the Enlightenment is won by everyone; the crowns say who won what. Carry on as free play if you like. | Game: every path has its crown, so the trader, the warlord and the builder can each say they won. Albert, Oct 29. |
| A caravan has a route | It loads goods at the Market, sells them at a trade stop (farther pays more, up to ×2.5; a wanted good ×1.5), can buy spice there, and repeats. Robbers attack carts near their camps unless a post, tower or soldier is close. Up to 12 soldiers can escort one: tap the caravan, Escort +4. | Merchants carried goods where they were scarce, and paid for guards. |
| You hold trade stops | Each of the two routes has four stops. Build a Trade post on a stop to hold it. You take a cut of the sales of every other side there, by how many of that route you hold: 1: 20%, 2: 25%, 3: 30%, 4: 40%. Hold all four of a route and it also pays 120 gold a minute. Up to 12 soldiers garrison each stop. You set what the stop wants. | Whoever held the road taxed the trade on it. |
| A building is placed | It is paid for and stands as a site at 0%. The nearest free villager walks there and builds it in the listed time; more builders are faster (two ≈ 1.6×). With nobody on it, it does not rise. A line of wall gets two builders who work down it. | Fog of war | You see what your people and your allies see; the rest of the map is as you last saw it, and the unseen is dark. An army out of sight reaches you by its noise or by a messenger, and the odds count only the soldiers you see, or the report of a spy under 2 minutes old. Spies stay hidden until something of yours is close. Night shortens sight; a wood blocks it, so you see its edge and nothing behind; in fog, snow or a blizzard nobody sees past the visibility, a tower no farther than a man. The computers play without fog: an assumption of the game, the one way they are not like you. Choose it on or off when you start a game. | Scouts earned their pay by what they saw first. |
| A wall or building stands in the way | People walk around it. If nothing can get through, soldiers attack the wall in the way; villagers wait. | Walls delay; they do not stop an army with time. |
| The game saves itself | Every 10 seconds, and when you leave the page, on this phone. Next time it offers Resume or New game. | You should never lose a game to a phone call. |
| Difficulty | It turns the minds of the computers, never their stores. Easy: they think every 2 s, wait longer to attack and to ally, and nobody comes for you before 6:00. Normal: every 1 s. Hard: twice a second, bolder, quicker to pounce on a side at war, and they ally against whoever leads the score. | No cheating: only how they think. |
| Winning and losing | Conquest: win when the Raiders, the Rivals and the Fortress have all fallen, or hold the Throne for its time; lose when your Town Center falls. Or hold a wonder for 8 minutes, or have the top score at the bell in a timed game. | A seat of power is what a state is. |
